How the EasyMesh optimizer works

An EasyMesh controller knows where every client is and can ask a client to move. What it does not have is a good answer to when, and where to. The optimizer is that answer, kept outside the controller: a process that watches the mesh, measures each client's other options, decides with a policy it can explain, moves one client at a time and checks that the move happened. This page follows one decision from the first measurement to the record of why it was made.

The question

A home mesh has a gateway and a few extenders, each with access points on 2.4, 5 and 6 GHz. Every phone and laptop picks one of them and, left alone, stays there. Clients are sticky: a laptop that joined the extender in the hall keeps it after it is carried to the bedroom, where the gateway would serve it twice as fast. The client cannot see the mesh as a whole. The mesh can.

EasyMesh gives the controller the means. Agents report which clients they serve and how well they hear them; the controller can ask other agents to listen for a client; and it can send a steering request that makes the serving access point suggest, through an 802.11v BSS Transition Management (BTM) request, that the client move to a named access point. The standard stops there. Which client to move, where, after how much evidence and how often, is left to whoever builds the controller. That is the optimizer's job.

What makes it hard

Measurements arrive late and not all at once. A client can refuse to move, or move somewhere else. Moving it costs a short interruption, and moving it back and forth costs more than leaving it. A good optimizer is as much about when not to act as about when to act.

Outside the controller

The labs keep a strict division. The EasyMesh implementation, RDK's or prplMesh's, supplies facts and mechanisms: topology, associations, link metrics, measurement queries and the steering transport. It supplies no target, no score, no threshold and no trigger, and the agents' own local steering is switched off for these experiments. Everything that is a decision lives in one external process, next to the controller in the lab VM.

Keeping the policy outside has a practical reason and a scientific one. Practically, one optimizer serves two different controllers. Scientifically, the same policy can run against the same rooms on both stacks, and the stack's own behaviour stops being mixed up with the policy's.

One loop

The optimizer is a loop that runs about once a second. Each turn observes, measures what is missing, decides for each client, passes the decision through the guardrails, acts on at most what they allow, and checks earlier actions. Every step writes to a journal.

Observing the mesh

The controller publishes its view through four reads: the topology (which agent serves which client, and the backhaul tree), the clients with their link metrics, the mesh devices, and the access points (BSSs) with their exact identities, bands and SSIDs. The optimizer keeps the raw records and turns them into one snapshot: an immutable picture of every client, its serving access point, its current link quality and when that was measured.

Two rules shape the snapshot. Everything carries its age, because a decision on a reading from a minute ago is a decision about a different house. And nothing unknown becomes a number: a missing measurement stays missing and can only ever stop an action, never cause one. The lab's medium knows the true signal of every link, and the optimizer never reads it; it sees only what a controller in a real house would see.

Asking about the other access points

The serving access point knows how well it hears its client. To compare, the optimizer needs to know how well the others would. EasyMesh has a message for this: Unassociated STA Link Metrics. The controller asks an agent's radio to listen, on its channel, for frames from a client it does not serve and report the client's signal, as RCPI. The optimizer asks through the controller, groups the clients per agent radio, and maps each answer to the exact access point (BSSID) that would serve the client there.

These queries are slow and serialized in a real controller: with twenty clients, a complete picture takes tens of seconds. So the optimizer collects in rounds of a few clients, keeps every answer with its age, and after a move asks again only about the radios the moved client still needs. A decision waits for a candidate picture that is complete and fresh enough; until then, it does nothing.

Signal in one ruler

EasyMesh reports signal as RCPI, an integer from 0 to 220 in half-decibel steps: RCPI 0 is −110 dBm and every 2 RCPI is 1 dB more. The default policy speaks in RCPI because that is what the controller reports, and two of its numbers do most of the work.

Deciding

The default policy is a threshold policy with memory. For each client, every turn, it asks: is the current link weak, is a fresh candidate clearly better, and has that been true long enough? A condition must hold for five seconds before it counts, so one bad sample moves nobody. A client that moved is left alone for at least twenty seconds of dwell, however the numbers look.

When more than one candidate qualifies, the strongest fresh one wins; the target is always an exact BSSID, never "somewhere on the gateway". The policy is plain data: a versioned file of thresholds and timers, with a digest recorded in the journal, so a run always says which policy made it.

Every "no" has a reason

Most turns end in no action, and that is the interesting part. Each decision carries a reason, and the journal keeps it:

The journal is JSON lines, each record carrying the hash of the one before it: the snapshot, the decision with its reason and scores, the action, the outcome. A run can be audited after the fact and replayed: the same snapshots through the same policy give the same decisions.

Steering

When a decision survives the guardrails, the optimizer hands the controller one request: steer this client to that BSSID. In the labs this goes through each lab's own steering command, a narrow, validated entry point; the optimizer never reaches into the controller's internals.

Checking that it worked

A steering request that was sent is not a client that moved. The optimizer counts a steer as successful only when the controller's model shows the client on the target and traffic flows there, within ten seconds. A client that lands on a different access point than the one asked for is reported as a failure at once, not after the timeout. A failure puts the client into backoff, sixty seconds after the first and doubling with each repeat, up to ten minutes, so a client that refuses to move is not asked every turn.

Why traffic counts

A controller's model can lag behind reality or keep a stale owner for a while. A client that passes traffic through the target has moved; a row in a table may not mean that yet.

Guardrails

Above the policy sits a safety layer that does not care how good a decision looks. In the room service that runs the optimizer live, it enforces:

  • at most one request in flight per client, and at least five seconds between requests for a client;
  • a ceiling on requests across the whole mesh, 300 a minute;
  • a pause for a client after three failures within three minutes;
  • a pause for a client that moved four times within a minute: an oscillation, which almost always means two policies or two measurements disagree;
  • health gates: no action while the mesh is unhealthy or the controller's inventory is incomplete.

A paused client stays paused until an operator resumes it; the room shows why it stopped.

Beyond signal

Signal strength on the same band is the default and the best-measured case. Other policies are opt-in, each with its own evidence rules:

  • Band upgrade moves a client from 2.4 to 5 GHz, or 5 to 6, when the target band is strong enough (RCPI 120 by default) and the loss small. Cross-band evidence cannot come from the same-band listening query, so these rooms use scans the client receives, comparing like with like.
  • Load-aware balancing moves a strong client from a busy channel to a fresh, quieter one. It reads the access points' own utilization reports, insists that both are recent and advancing, and never treats a missing load as zero.
  • The counter guard vetoes balancing when native retry, transmit-failure or receive-drop rates say a link is struggling, whatever the load says.
  • Pre-association recommendation suggests where a joining client should land, bounded in time and probes, with a 2.4 GHz failsafe.

Two stacks, one optimizer

The policy, the planners, the verifier and the journal are shared. What differs between the RDK controller and prplMesh is one adapter each, named in one table:

StackObserves throughSteers through
RDKthe controller's web API (topology, clients, devices, BSSs, the unassociated-STA query)the lab's steering command into the controller
prplMesha topology adapter and the controller's northbound data model (NBAPI)the lab's steering command into prplMesh

Both labs pin the optimizer at the same path, next to the RF medium they also pin. When a policy behaves differently on the two stacks, the difference is in the stacks, which is often exactly what the experiment set out to find.

The room service

An optimizer needs something to optimize. In the labs that is a room: one of the medium's compiled worlds, with clients walking, crowds arriving, extenders failing. The room service, the optimizer repository's second package, plays a room through the medium in time, keeps the clients' containers associated and on their bands, runs the optimizer against the controller while the room plays, and serves the live view.

The room service never decides for the optimizer and never shortcuts the controller: the optimizer in a room sees exactly what it would see in a house, and acts through exactly the same command.

Proving a policy

A policy is accepted in steps, from cheap to live:

  1. Units and replay. The decision engine is tested on its own, and recorded runs replay to identical decisions.
  2. A test double. For algorithm work without a lab, simulate turns a compiled world into synthetic, controller-shaped snapshots through a declared sensor model. Its records say they are simulated, and they never count as evidence.
  3. A case matrix. Ten golden worlds, five traffic profiles, the policy configurations and seeds expand into a hash-verified matrix; a case a lab cannot run yet is an explicit blocker, not a silent skip.
  4. Live acceptance. The acceptance tools run against a lab: readiness (every client on its best access point at rest), the room catalog in a browser, the geometry rooms where extenders lose and change their backhaul, RF, load, counter and traffic checks. Both labs' suites call them from here.

The progression in a lab mirrors that: observe only, then recommend and review, then allow one explicit action with its verification and backoff, and only then let it run a room.
